# Character sheet

The character page is a **dossier**, not a form to complete. Fill the top
(Identity & core engine) properly and you have a character; everything below is
optional depth you add only when it will change something on the page. A field
that never affects a scene is dead weight, so empty sections stay collapsed
until you open them.

Every field is stored on the character and, when filled, flows into the AI
features that draw on story-world context — **Ask the book**, **Talk to
{Name}**, the consistency **Audit**, and the character's Story-Bible card in
retrieval. Nothing you write here is required, and nothing is shown to readers;
it's your working memory for the character.

> **Fill from book** (button in the page header) drafts many of these fields
> for you from the scenes that actually feature the character — one-liner,
> motivation, arc, backstory, and the identity basics. It reads the prose,
> proposes values, and lets you review every one before anything saves. Use it
> once a character has scenes linked to them.

---

## The hero row

- **Role** — a short label ("Detective", "Prism", "Guild handler").
- **Gender** / **Pronouns** — free text. Whatever fits your story. Surfaced to
  every AI feature that builds a character profile, so generated prose uses the
  right pronouns instead of guessing.
- **Age** — numeric.
- **Life status** — Alive · Deceased · Missing · Unknown. Keeps AI features from
  writing a character as still alive after they die mid-book.
- **Main character** — promotes them to the inner ring of the Relations graph.
  For protagonists, not everyone.
- **One-liner** — not a summary of traits, a summary of *tension*. "A pacifist
  who keeps ending up in fights he could have prevented."

Below the hero row: **Tags** (freeform, project-wide typeahead) and **Also known
as** (the other names this character goes by — aliases feed speaker attribution,
search, and every AI feature that needs to recognise them).

## Identity & core engine

The engine of the character. Fill this before anything else.

- **Class origin → now** — where they started and where they are. The gap
  between the two is characterization.
- **Education** — formal or otherwise. This and class are voice inputs: they set
  vocabulary, assumptions, and what the character notices.
- **Wants** (external) — what they are concretely pursuing. Must be checkable:
  you can point at the page and say "achieved" or "failed".
- **Needs** (internal) — what would actually make them whole. Usually invisible
  to them, and often the opposite of the strategy they use to chase the want.
- **Lie they believe** — the misbelief driving their bad decisions ("love must
  be earned", "safety requires control").
- **Truth they meet** — the truth the arc drives them toward.
- **Core fear** — what they organize their life to avoid. Want drives pursuit;
  fear drives avoidance — scenes need both engines. The fear is usually the
  lie's shadow.
- **Where the lie began** — write it as a *scene*, not a summary. Where were
  they, who said what, what did they decide about the world in that moment?
- **Central contradiction** — two things that are both true about them and don't
  fit. "Brutally honest with strangers; lies constantly to the people she
  loves." This is where the character stops being a type. If you can't find one,
  the character isn't done.
- **Values under pressure** — pick three values and order them (loyalty > truth >
  safety). When a scene forces a choice, the higher value wins — until the arc's
  crisis tests the ranking itself.
- **Decision heuristic** — "Under pressure, they choose ___ over ___." One line
  that mechanically generates in-character choices when you're stuck.
- **Stakes** — what specifically breaks, for them and for others, if they fail
  to get the want. If nothing breaks, they don't belong in the story.

## Arc

Frame the arc as evidence for and against the lie, not as plot slots.

- **Beginning** — the lie, operationalized as behavior.
- **Midpoint** — the moment the lie first fails to explain something, or the
  scene where lie and truth demand opposite actions.
- **End** — adopted the truth, doubled down on the lie (a negative arc), or never
  needed to change and instead changed the world (a flat arc).

## Voice & presence

How they sound and move — the calibration set.

- **Accent** / **Vocabulary** / **Speech tic** — the surface texture of their
  speech.
- **Sample line — calm / angry / lying** — write these three. They're the
  calibration set an AI feature (or you) matches when writing the character's
  dialogue.
- **Register** — formal ↔ casual; where do they sit, and when do they slip?
- **Rhythm** — clipped or flowing; do they interrupt; do they trail off?
- **Forbidden words** — words this character would *never* say. As useful as the
  tells.
- **Subtext habit** — deflects with jokes; answers questions with questions;
  agrees then does the opposite.
- **Humor style** — dry / absent / cruel / self-deprecating. "No humor" is a
  choice too.
- **Languages** — what they speak, and who they can understand that others
  can't. That's a plot capability.
- **How they occupy space** — posture, gait, stillness or motion; one or two
  lines a scene can borrow anytime.
- **Presentation** — what their appearance *choices* signal, not what they look
  like.
- **Baseline & stress tells** — their normal physical state, and how being
  rattled, afraid, or lying leaks through. The tell only reads if the baseline
  is established.

> **Tip — register shifts.** A strong exercise the sheet doesn't force into
> fields: note how the character speaks to a superior, to someone with less
> power, to an intimate, to a stranger, to an enemy. The "less power" row is the
> reveal — how they treat a waiter, a junior, a child.

## Function in the story

What the character is *for* in the cast. Every major character should answer the
story's central question differently — that's what a cast is.

- **Thematic argument** — the position this character embodies in the story's
  central question.
- **Relation to protagonist** — opposition / mirror / temptation / ally /
  measuring stick, and in what way.
- **Self-image** / **Public persona** / **Private truth** — who they believe
  they are, who they perform being, who they actually are. The gaps between the
  three are engines: self-image vs. truth is dramatic irony; persona vs. truth
  is the mask that slips.
- **Buttons** — what reliably provokes them, to anger or shame or delight. Levers
  other characters can pull; every button is a scene waiting to happen.
- **Allegiances & obligations** — groups, families, causes they belong to, and
  what membership *demands*. Obligations that conflict with the want are free
  plot.
- **How they escalate** — aggression, withdrawal, sabotage, weaponized
  competence, going over heads.
- **Cornered behavior** — what they do when they have no good options. The moment
  readers learn who they really are.

## Capabilities & limits

A genre module — skip it for realist fiction.

- **Abilities** — magic, tech, training, powers; what can they actually do?
- **Costs** — what each use takes: energy, sanity, time, moral weight,
  resources.
- **Hard limits** — what they cannot do, ever. Limits generate more plot than
  powers; readers track limits, not strengths.
- **Conditions** — what the ability requires: tools, materials, states,
  permissions.
- **Who knows** — who is aware of these capabilities.

## Continuity ledger

Facts that must never drift.

- **Physical constants** — only features that appear on the page (a scar, a limp
  in cold weather, the tattoo with a story). If it's never mentioned, it doesn't
  go here.
- **Conditions & disabilities** — anything that changes what's possible in a
  scene: mobility, stamina, perception, medication, triggers. Scene-logistics
  facts, not traits.
- **Timeline anchors** — birth year and the dates of any event referenced
  in-story. Do the math once, write it down, never re-derive it.
- **Knows at story start** / **Doesn't know** / **Believes wrongly** — the single
  most common source of continuity errors and POV leaks is a character acting on
  information they haven't received yet.
- **Secrets** — one per line: the secret, who else knows, what forces the
  reveal.
- **Possessions with story weight** — objects that will matter (the ring, the
  dead brother's watch), and where each one is.

---

## Backstory & depth

Fill only what will surface on the page.

- **Backstory** — past facts the story establishes about them, before it began.
- **Regrets** — a strong prompt: regret is compressed backstory plus motivation
  in one line, and it doesn't have to be the core wound.
- **Family / upbringing.**
- **Skills & ceiling** — note the ceiling too: how capable, so solutions stay
  fair to the reader.
- **Routines & habits** — useful mainly for disruption; an established routine
  broken tells the reader something is wrong.
- **Appearance beyond the constants**, and **Tastes & quirks.**

---

## Appears in scenes · Mentioned in prose

Two reverse indexes at the bottom of the page:

- **Appears in scenes** — every scene that has linked this character via its
  Links panel.
- **Mentioned in prose** — every scene where the character's name was inserted
  with `@`, with a snippet and a mention count.
