# Plot and time

JustWrite has four planning surfaces that all touch on plot and chronology, and the differences between them are worth understanding before you start using them. Two of them — Narrative strands and the Plot Board — are about **story shape**. The other two — per-entity Events and the project Timeline — are about **world chronology**.

This page explains each in turn, and (importantly) when you would use one vs. another.

---

## Narrative strands — the short answer

A **narrative strand** is a continuous thread that runs through your manuscript and has its own internal shape — a beginning, turning points, and a resolution.

That's an intentionally broad definition. Different writers draw the same map differently. In practice, a strand is whatever you decide is worth tracking as a through-line.

Common examples:

- **The main plot** — protagonist vs. antagonist
- **A romantic subplot**
- **A character's internal arc** — e.g. "Elena moves from denial to acceptance"
- **A mystery thread** — clues planted, the reveal
- **A thematic spine** — a motif or question the book keeps returning to
- **A secondary POV character's independent plot**

A typical book has two to six strands. The test: **"Does this thread have its own turning points?"** If yes, it deserves its own strand so you can map those turning points to chapters.

## Strands view — planning one thread in detail

The **Strands** view (Manuscript section in the sidebar) is the dedicated notebook page for a single strand. You open one strand at a time.

### What's on the page

- **Strand name** — inline-editable header.
- **Blurb** — a one-or-two-sentence italic summary. Useful as a quick reminder of what the strand is about without opening the full notes.
- **Colour swatch picker** — ten preset colours. The chosen colour flows through to the strand's row label on the Plot Board and to the left-border accent on every beat card.
- **Status** — same status palette used everywhere else (Draft, Revise, Done…).
- **Rich-text notes body** — a full prose editor. Write as much as you want: a synopsis for this thread, notes on each character involved, decisions you've made and why. This is your long-form planning document for the strand.
- **Beats** — labelled turning-point markers pinned to specific chapters or scenes. Each beat has a label (e.g. "Midpoint"), an optional note, and a scene assignment. Beats are sorted automatically in story order. Preset autocomplete suggestions on the label field: Inciting, First turn, Midpoint, Crisis, Climax, Resolution, Setup, Reveal, Setback, Refusal.
- **"Appears in scenes"** — a read-only index of every scene that has been tagged with this strand via its Links panel. Click to jump to the scene.
- **Groups** — strands can be grouped alongside characters and locations.

### How to use it

Use Strands view when you are **planning a single thread in depth**. The notes body is where you think through:

- What does this arc need to accomplish?
- Where does it start, where does it end, what has to happen in the middle?
- Which characters are involved? What changes?

Use beats to mark the structural skeleton once you know roughly where the thread turns. Use the scenes list to **audit coverage** — if your romance subplot has zero scenes tagged for four chapters in a row, that's a gap worth addressing.

## Plot Board — the wide-angle view

The **Plot Board** (Planning section in the sidebar) is a two-axis grid:

- **Rows** are narrative strands.
- **Columns** are chapters (plus an **Unassigned** column on the left for beats that aren't yet placed in any chapter).

Each beat appears as a small card at the strand × chapter intersection, with the strand's colour as a left border.

### What you can do

- **Click an empty cell** to add a new beat in that strand and chapter immediately.
- **Click a beat** to edit its label, note, or scene pin.
- **Drag a beat** to move it across chapters or strands. (If you drop into a different chapter, the scene pin is cleared, since scenes belong to chapters.)
- **Drag a strand label** to reorder the rows.
- **Rename a strand** inline by clicking its name in the row header.
- **Delete a beat** with the X that appears on hover (with an undo toast).

### Plot structure templates

There's an **Apply template** dropdown that creates a new strand pre-loaded with the beats from one of five standard structural frameworks:

| Template | Beats | When to use |
|---|---|---|
| **Three-Act Structure** | 7 | The universal scaffold. Works for almost anything. |
| **Five-Act Structure** | 7 (Freytag's pyramid) | Tragedies and ensemble dramas. |
| **Save the Cat** | 15 (Blake Snyder) | Tight, popular for genre fiction (thriller, romance, comedy). |
| **Hero's Journey** | 12 (Campbell / Vogler) | Transformational arcs, fantasy, myth. |
| **Story Circle** | 8 (Dan Harmon) | Compact; works at the chapter level or the arc level. |

When you apply one, all the beats start in the **Unassigned** column. You drag them onto chapters as you decide where they land.

### How to use it

The Plot Board is for asking **structural questions**:

- **Which chapters are doing all the heavy lifting?** (Look for chapters with beats from many strands.)
- **Where is the midpoint of my romance subplot relative to my main plot's midpoint?** (Should they coincide? Or deliberately not?)
- **Does Chapter 9 have beats from three different strands converging? Is that the climax I intended?**

It is also where you spot **structural gaps**: a stretch of chapters with no beats in any strand is probably a sagging middle.

Apply a template when starting a new project, or when you suspect a structural problem and want the standard turning-point names in front of you as a checklist.

## Strands vs. Plot Board — they share the same data

Important: Strands view and the Plot Board are **two views of the same data**, not two separate systems. Every strand you create appears as a row in the Plot Board. Every beat you add in one view appears in the other.

The difference is your angle of approach:

- **Strands view** = close-up on one strand. Notes + beats.
- **Plot Board** = wide-angle on all strands. The grid.

Use whichever lens fits the question you're asking right now.

## Per-entity Events — biographies and place histories

A separate concept entirely.

Every character, location, object, group, and the world Setting has its own **Events timeline**, accessible from an **Events** button on the entity's detail page. An event has:

- A date / time (optional — undated events fall to the bottom of the timeline)
- A title (required)
- An optional note

Events are biographical and historical records:

- For a **character**: their life history. *"Born in Argos, 1842. Father dies, 1856. Joins the merchant guild, 1860. First voyage, 1861."*
- For a **location**: the place's history. *"Founded 1720. Great fire of 1854. Rebuilt in 1860."*
- For an **object**: an artefact's provenance.
- For the **Setting** (a singleton entry under the Architecture view): world-level events that don't belong to any one entity.

### Why a writer would use this

Events are most valuable when your story has **dates that matter**:

- Historical fiction
- Multi-generational sagas
- Time-travel
- Mysteries with carefully placed past events
- Anything where your characters' arrival, departure, or birth dates need to line up

If your novel takes place in a single afternoon, you probably don't need events. If it spans decades, you'll thank yourself for filing them.

## The Timeline view — your world's history

The **Timeline** view (Planning section in the sidebar) is a read-only **merged** view of every event from every entity, sorted chronologically.

Each row shows the weekday, date, time, title, optional note, and an icon badge identifying who or what the event belongs to. Click the title to edit the event; click the badge to jump to the entity's page.

### When to use it

Use the Timeline when you need to **audit chronology**:

- "Did Aria arrive in Dunmore before or after the flood?"
- "When the regent died, where was each of these characters?"
- "Did this character leave before they arrived?" (Sounds silly, but it happens. Especially in revision.)

You add events from the entity's page; the Timeline collects them automatically. There is no manual entry on the Timeline itself.

## Putting them all together

The four planning surfaces and when to use each:

| Question you're asking | Best surface |
|---|---|
| *"What is this subplot about and where does it turn?"* | **Strands view** — prose notes + beats |
| *"How do all my threads weave across all chapters?"* | **Plot Board** — the grid |
| *"What happened in my story world and when?"* | **Timeline** (project-wide) or the entity's Events timeline |
| *"I need a known structural framework laid over my outline."* | **Plot Board → Apply template** |
| *"Did this character arrive before the war started?"* | **Timeline** — chronological events |
| *"How many chapters of romance subplot have I actually written?"* | **Strand → Appears in scenes** list |

Most writers use **strands and the plot board heavily** and reach for **events and the timeline** only when chronology matters.

## A practical workflow

A realistic order:

1. **Apply a template** to your Plot Board to get a main-plot strand with the canonical beats. (Or skip this if you prefer to design your own structure.)
2. **Add one or two subplot strands.** A romance, a thematic question, a character arc.
3. **For each strand, write a short blurb** so you know what it is when you come back next week.
4. **Drag beats onto chapters** as you decide where they land. Leave beats in **Unassigned** until you're ready.
5. **Tag scenes with strands** via the Links panel as you write. This populates the "Appears in scenes" list on each strand and feeds the Plot Board.
6. **(If chronology matters)** Add events to your main characters as you write — birth dates, arrivals, departures, deaths. Check the project Timeline occasionally for contradictions.
